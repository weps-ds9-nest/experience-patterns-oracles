# I’ll be honest I’m conflicted when it comes to permissions, as an end user I get a sense of comfort from knowing that an app can’t access the everything on my device without my permissions, but as a designer I have often seen them as yet another hurdle to cross.

I’ll be honest I’m conflicted when it comes to permissions, as an end user I get a sense of comfort from knowing that an app can’t access the everything on my device without my permissions, but as a designer I have often seen them as yet another hurdle to cross.

It results in the same conversations I’ve had all too many times and ends with statements like:

> “What happens if they don’t let us access their photos?! It’ll break the whole app!”
> 
> 
> “If we can’t send notifications then we’ll never keep engagement up!”

All too often the gut reaction when designing an app is to leave the developers to shove the permissions in and hope for the best. Perhaps with an empty promise to review it at a later date. It’s not something you can afford to gloss over either, especially if the permissions are critical to the app’s success.

Ok, you get it, it’s a pain in the ass, but handling it properly doesn’t have to be. Here’s a few rules that have helped me keep users clicking that accept button and reduce the overall stress of the whole experience.

### 1. First and foremost make the user aware of the value you’re providing

For critical permissions this could be done before the user ever opens the app via marketing and the app store. In other cases it could be at a point where the user has decided they want to take an action within the app, and all they would need is a little clarification screen. Whatever the case go out of your way to explain the value and give them a real reason to click accept.

WhatsApp: They fail pretty hard out of the gate on this. Immediately upon opening the app you get greeted by a request for your contacts with only a short in notification explanation as to why. Props to them for being upfront about being uploaded to their server, but if I'm honest that just lowers the chances of me clicking ok. Whats worse is when you click “Don’t Allow” you simply get the welcome screen and nothing is mentioned again, implying it wasn’t all that import in the first place.

### 2. Always account for them saying no

It might seem the worst possible outcome, but its likely to happen. Ok so your phone app no longer functions without mic access? At least tell the user and give them some steps to correct it. Not allowed to send notifications? Prompt them occasionally about what they have missed by disabling it. Whatever the permissions and end result it’s always best to bite to bullet and put in the work to change the users mind.

Google Maps: Ok so it falls into the same trap as WhatsApp, although it is more self explanatory why they are asking. The impressive thing though is what happens if you don’t allow them access to your location; the app continues to work to the best of its ability without it, even to the point of allowing directions from one place to another. It’s only at the point that you try to interact with a feature that depends on location that it prompts you again. 10/10 Google ⭐️

### 3. Context is king

Waiting for the user to try and complete an action before prompting for the permissions is a sure fire way to get them to accept. It’s a careful balance though, you don’t want to frustrate them by getting in their way, so make sure the process is a smooth as possible. It’s also only useful for a permission that’s tied to a specific feature or function, so some design gymnastics might be required to pull it off effectively.

Things 3: Despite messing up with notifications by asking you straight away with little explanation, Things 3 pulls off getting access to your calendar superbly. They wait until you start to engage with the pages of the app that depends on it, and phrase in a friendly enough manner that it becomes a no brainer. Hell I clicked Show Events without thinking about it just to see what would happen.

### 4. Keep it conversational

Especially if the value in giving the permission isn’t clear, making it part of a conversation with the user makes it more palatable. Turning it into a conversation gives you longer to explain the value and gain the users trust. When doing this as part of the on-boarding process those extra few seconds of familiarity can make all the difference.

Sip: Product Hunts News app gets close by asking for permissions during the on-boarding process. They do a good job at making you aware of why they’re asking to send you notifications and the benefit to you, but they fall down slightly on the execution.

### 5. If in doubt spin it

This could be as simple as “we can keep you up-to date with the latest information” or “so we can share progress with friends”. Yes sometimes the underlying reason for asking a user for a permission is self serving, but there should be at least some value for them to do it, otherwise you’re tricking yourself thinking anyone would realistically click it.

Headspace: They’re generally fantastic at selling you the benefits of the app, and this doesn't stop when it comes to permissions. They successfully spin what is essentially a way of keeping you engaged with their app as a selfless attempt to keep you on track with your meditation goals. Even holding off asking you until you have properly engaged with the app. Sadly they do stumble at the last hurdle by throwing two permission requests at you in one go (one even overriding the other.)
