# The UX of Waiting: Designing for Loading States

The UX of Waiting: Designing for Loading States

Imagine this:

You’re ordering food from your favorite delivery app. It’s late. You’re tired. Your stomach is staging a protest. You tap “Order Now”… and then the app just… spins.

And spins.

And spins…. And spins…………..

No progress bar. No message. Just a blank, spinning icon that feels like a glitchy eternity.

After a minute, you give up and close the app. Frustrated, hungry, and more likely to never use it again.

## So what just happened?

The loading state, one of the tiniest, most overlooked moments in design, just broke a user’s trust.

And here’s the twist: the user _might not even care_ that it’s slow. What they really care about is not knowing what’s going on.

## Why Waiting UX Matters

Waiting isn’t the problem. Uncertainty is.

A study by the [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.linkedin.com/company/nielsen-norman-group/) found that users perceive waits to be shorter when progress indicators are used, even if the actual time is the same.

People can forgive slowness. But they won’t forgive silence.

## Great Products Design for the Wait

Let’s look at this Case Study:

## 🎥 Netflix

When you’re waiting for a show to load, Netflix often plays trailers or previews. You’re not just staring at a spinner, you’re being entertained.

**Pre-roll previews for Netflix Originals**: Netflix has indeed implemented and continues to test and refine pre-roll trailers for its own original content. These are often personalized based on your viewing history and preferences, aiming to introduce you to other shows or movies you might like within their catalog. This means instead of just a loading spinner, you might see a 15–30 second preview of an upcoming or existing Netflix title.

**User Experience Improvement**: The goal behind this is exactly as the statement suggests: to entertain users and reduce the perceived waiting time. Instead of an idle loading screen, you’re given a taste of other content, which can enhance the overall user experience and potentially lead to discovering new shows.

**Intentional UX Design**: Netflix engineered the home screen to preload and start short previews as soon as you hover or highlight a title, even before the full content plays. The goal is to immerse users in the mood, tone, and characters of a show, reducing perceived wait times and increasing engagement.

> **_That’s smart UX. It distracts, informs, and engages._**

## What Bad Loading UX Looks Like

*   Blank screens
*   Spinners with no message
*   No feedback on what’s happening
*   Repetitive reload loops

These lead to:

*   Higher bounce rates
*   Canceled actions
*   Lost trust

## What to Do Instead

Designing for the UX of waiting is about transparency + reassurance.

Here are a few practices that work:

1.   ✅ Use progress indicators (bars, steps, %)
2.   ✅ Show real-time feedback (e.g., “Uploading photo…”)
3.   ✅ Entertain or educate during long waits
4.   ✅ Keep your tone friendly, not robotic
5.   ✅ Use skeletal loaders or placeholders to show movement

## Final Thought

_Waiting is inevitable._

But we can turn that moment into something delightful, informative, or even human.

Because at the end of the day, UX isn’t just about speed, it’s about _trust_.

> _Design like people are waiting because they are._
