I am a UX designer, which means I can no longer use the internet without noticing everything that is wrong with it.

This article is about UX patterns that are frustrating, widely adopted, and somehow still treated as acceptable at massive scale. If you have never noticed them, consider yourself lucky. Once you do, they become impossible to unsee. Your tolerance for digital nonsense may permanently decrease after reading this article. That is your warning!

Before getting into the list, one clarification. Many of these patterns exist for business, legal, or regulatory reasons. It is an argument that they make the experience worse, and that the world would be a slightly better place if they were not treated as default solutions.

### 1. Cookie consent banners that hijack the page

Cookie consent banners are legally required, but their current execution is one of the most disruptive patterns on the web. Cookie consent banners became mandatory due to privacy regulations such as the EU’s GDPR and the California Consumer Privacy Act, introduced by lawmakers to limit uncontrolled data tracking and give users legal rights over how their data is collected and used. However they often block content entirely, appear before any meaningful interaction, and demand a decision from users who have not even had a chance to understand what they are consenting to.

As a result, users stop reading, stop thinking, and click “Accept” just to make the interface go away. A pattern designed to protect users ends up conditioning them to ignore it.

Full-screen cookie modal that blocks content before any interaction.

### 2. E-commerce pop-ups that block the product you came to see

You have probably been there. You are scrolling Instagram and the algorithm works almost too well. It shows you the exact product you only thought about (No, you did not say it out loud. I believe you).

You click, ready to see the product. Instead, a cookie banner appears before the image loads. You tap “Accept” because at this point we have all surrendered, haven’t we?

Then a pop-up asks you to sign up for emails. You close it. You try to scroll. Another pop-up appears offering 10–20% off. You close it too. You finally reach the product images, start to engage, click on a related item, and the same pop-up appears again. By now, you are no longer excited.

Interruptive sign-up modal at a high-intent moment.

### 3. Registration walls

You search for something specific, find the perfect article, click the link, read the title, and immediately hit an unskippable registration wall (looking at you, NY times). Instantly.

You are asked to create an account or pay to read 100 words you came for. Yes, it is a business model. That does not make it any less irritating.

If content is behind a paywall, users should know that before they click. Medium does this well by clearly labeling member-only articles in search results. If you are not a member and not willing to pay, you do not waste time or emotional energy. Google, take notes!

Account required before content access.

### 4. Ads everywhere, all at once, all the time

You try to read an article and are immediately surrounded by ads. One on the left, one on the right, one at the top, one in the middle of the content, and a video ad auto-playing in the corner for no reason at all.

At this point, reading becomes work. Your attention is constantly pulled away from the thing you came for, and the experience feels chaotic rather than informative.

Ads are often necessary to support free content. But when they dominate the layout and compete with the content itself, they defeat their own purpose. If the interface feels like it is fighting for your attention something has gone seriously wrong.

Ads surrounding the content from every possible direction.

### 5. Ads that look like content

Some ads do not look like ads at all. They are intentionally designed to blend in with real content, mimicking headlines, layouts, and even typography. This is a _deceptive pattern_ often referred to as _disguised ads_.

### 6. Forced “What’s New” Pages After Updates

The “Update Chrome” button sits in the top corner of the browser and does not go away until you click it. Eventually, you do. The browser restarts and immediately opens a new tab with a “What’s new” article.

When users click update, they are signaling one thing only: finish the update. Opening extra pages pulls attention away from the task they were already doing. Updates should return users to their previous context.

What’s New in Chrome article.

### 7. “Watch an ad to continue” interruptions

You are in the middle or just at the beginning, of a game, a video, or an article, and everything stops. To continue, you are required to watch an ad. Sometimes 30 seconds. Sometimes several in a row. No skipping.

On a recent flight, I tried to start a movie and was greeted by five unskippable ads before the film even began. Apparently, watching the movie required additional data collection, brand exposure, and a test of my patience.

### 8. Forced app updates

You open an app to do something simple and are immediately redirected to the App Store. An update is required. You wait, the app reloads, and sometimes you are asked to log in again.

This usually happens for security patches, API changes, or backend compatibility issues. All valid reasons.

From a user perspective, it is still disruptive. In many cases, this could have happened automatically in the background instead of at the exact moment the user needed the app. If an update is mandatory, it should be as invisible as possible. Blocking access should be the last option, not the default.

Forced app update that blocks basic usage.

### 9. Hiding prices behind a contact form

You are interested in a product or service and want to see the price to decide if it is even worth considering. Instead of a number, you get a form.

Email, phone number, zip code, and possibly your pet’s name and family history. At the end, a message appears saying, “We will contact you.”

What follows is a sales call where you repeat the same information, ask for the price again (they wouldn’t tell you), and after a 15-minute lecture finally learn that the product is wildly overpriced for your needs. A single number could have saved everyone this time.

This pattern is known as price obfuscation or lead capture gating. It prioritizes sales metrics over user efficiency and replaces transparency with friction.

### 10. Unsubscribing that requires logging in

This is self-explanatory. You receive a marketing email and decide to unsubscribe. Reasonable. You click the link and are taken to a page that asks you to log in before you can stop the emails.

At this point, the experience starts feeling hostile. If you remember your password, you waste time. If you do not, you reset it just to escape more emails and waste more time. If a system can send you emails, it can stop sending them without requiring authentication. Anything else feels like deliberate friction.

There are dozens more UX patterns that frustrate me as both a designer and a user.

At first, these things seem minor. One pop-up, one extra step, one unnecessary interruption. But they stack up. Eventually, they push you to that familiar moment where you seriously consider throwing your phone across the room, yelling at your laptop, or both.

I am not sure if anyone has quantified how much time these patterns waste, but it is worth thinking about. All these tiny interruptions steal something far more valuable than attention. Time.
