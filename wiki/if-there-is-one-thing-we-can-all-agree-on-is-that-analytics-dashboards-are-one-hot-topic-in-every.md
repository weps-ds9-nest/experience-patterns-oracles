If there is one thing we can all agree on is that Analytics Dashboards are one hot topic in every company — and in the analytics community itself. The truth is that dashboards should not be the only approach to drive a data-driven culture, but sometimes they are unavoidable.

The power of a dashboard is indeed limited, but a well-designed (and informative) one can save time from both analysts and stakeholders when exploring data and making recurrent decisions based on specific metrics.

And there is a lot to be considered when creating one.

It goes without saying that no dashboard can be considered merely good if it does not include the right KPIs — but this is not the focus of this article. Assuming all relevant KPIs and metrics were chosen, **what else is needed to ensure a dashboard is, as said, “well-designed”?**

The list is endless, but here goes five (IMO, golden) steps you can follow in order to deliver a high-quality one –

1.   Get to know your audience and their data routines
2.   Guide them through the story behind the data
3.   Go for the powerful (but not necessarily complex) visualisation
4.   Design with cleanness and consistency, and drive accessibility
5.   Provide meaningful information for data interpretation

Now, let’s deep dive into each of them.

## It all starts with people (and how they behave)

_Get to know your audience and their data routines_

Designing a dashboard requires beyond everything related to the data itself, a deep understanding of its audience characteristics and behaviours.

Even before picking the KPIs, it is important that you get familiar with the its primary audience and how they intend to — or should — use it.

Here are some questions you can ask to dig a little deeper into it:

*   Who is the primary audience of it?
*   What is the purpose of this dashboard — is it to present or explore data?
*   How often will users monitor this data?
*   How do users expect to make decisions based on the data presented?
*   Which questions do they expect to answer through this dashboard?
*   What is the data literacy level of the audience?
*   What is their graphicacy level?

On the graphicacy level topic, ask the stakeholders to share the reports they are used to create or read; and other dashboards they often look at. This should help you get a sense of what types of visuals they are used to.

**It doesn’t mean that you should follow their preferences or stick to what they know.** Instead, it can help you choose the right technique (the one that delivers the insight intuitively) and maybe at the same time increase their graphicacy level.

_E.g. It is very common to find stakeholders who like Pie Charts; knowing that enables you to present alternatives and explain why and how a different visual approach could be more effective._

At the end of this process you should have enough information about the context of which the dashboard will be part of — from audience to routines.

> **Side note**
> 
> 
> It is possible that going through this process you reach the conclusion that the context itself should change so that the audience can make the most of the data.
> 
> 
> E.g. Imagine stakeholders request a dashboard that will support on a monthly business KPIs review — and that they are not monitoring some of these KPIs more frequently. If you are confident that they should monitor it more often; this is the moment you identify the opportunity to change the context. You could then propose a different set up for the dashboard requested or even design an alternative one that would facilitate the adoption of a new data routine.

## Go with the (thought-through) flow

_Guide the audience through the story behind the data_

Once you have picked the correct KPIs and know your audience, it is time to plan the dashboard.

Just like telling any story, planning a dashboard means thinking how to best position the different pieces of information. In this case, charts, in a systemised manner.

### Information hierarchy: Inverted Pyramid

The first concept that can help you on that is the [Inverted Pyramid](https://vanseodesign.com/web-design/inverted-pyramid-design/) which adapted to a dashboard information hierarchy means that –

*   the top of the dashboard includes the **most significant data**;
*   the middle includes **relevant plots of your data**;
*   and the bottom includes **contextual (or background) data**.

For “data” you can understand **a single chart or even a set of charts** — as some dashboards are more effective if structured by sections or tabs. These sections or tabs can also follow the same Inverted Pyramid approach within.

### Five Second Rule

Another famous concept is the Five Second Rule which states that the **most relevant information should be found in five seconds or less**.

The choice of “most relevant information” should be driven by the **primary and/or secondary business questions the dashboard general audience will have in mind most of the time when looking at it**.

As an example that serves both concepts presented above, one of my favourite tactics is to position Scorecards (or “big numbers”) at the very top of the entire dashboard. These scorecards display KPIs and informational metrics, delivering the “most relevant information” quickly and providing an at-a-glance view of the story behind the data.

## The beauty and effectiveness of simplicity

_Go for the powerful (but not necessarily complex) visualisation_

In the realm of data visualisation there is a lot that can be explored in order to communicate data in a beautiful, functional, and intuitive fashion. Finding the balance on it requires attention to details of each and every single piece of information and visual aids included in any report, analysis or dashboard.

> “Excellence in statistical graphics consists of complex ideas communicated with clarity, precision, and efficiency.” — Edward Tuftle

The definition of Graphical Excellence by Edward Tuftle summarises the ideal approach to be followed when presenting any data. To reach graphical excellence you should –

1.   **Clarity —** make sure the visualisation sticks to its purpose (description, exploration, tabulation or decoration) and presents the data clearly, leading the audience to think of its substance instead of the visual;
2.   **Precision** — ensure the it drives the correct interpretation of the data and avoids distorting the story behind it;
3.   **Efficiency** — present as much information as possible in the least space and ink possible. In other words, what is not necessary has to be removed to avoid confusion and/or redundancy.

## Excellence is in every detail

_Design with cleanness and consistency, and drive accessibility_

Designing a dashboard with graphical excellence means putting together a cohesive collection of charts designed by the same principles, which might seem too abstract at first.

Here goes a few tactical tips that can help you reach graphical excellence through the details –

**White spaces —** a dashboard should never deliver a **_“Where’s Wally?”_** experience. Distribute the information respecting white spaces so they can “shine” separately and effectively communicate their piece of the story.

**Filters —** sometimes a powerful set of filters is the key to help you achieve cleanliness (and scalability) in your dashboard. Which use cases of the dashboard can be powered by the usage of different filters?

**Chart titles —** neglecting chart titles can be a big mistake. In summary, a good chart title is a clean and concise answer to the _“What am I seeing here?”_ question that come to mind when one first sees it.

**Legends** — the presence of legends is indispensable to ensure that every visual aid of the chart is properly understood. Make sure colours, size, shape, and textures are always accompanied by legends.

**Grids, Axes, Labels, and Ticks —** these four can assist the understanding of the metrics, values, units of measure, scale and proportions. Be careful and apply them wisely or you might end up polluting the chart and creating redundancy.

**Colours** — we could for sure go much deeper in this one, but here goes a brief summary of things to have in mind when choosing the colour palette

*   **1) Culture** — not every culture reads colours the same way. Because of that, with the dashboard’s audience demographics in mind try exploring [how they would read the colours](https://informationisbeautiful.net/visualizations/colours-in-cultures/) and choose accordingly;
*   **2) Context** — the colour should assist the audience in understanding what the data means and that depends very much on its context E.g. negative growth shouldn’t always be presented in red — e.g. for _Avg Delivery Time_ it could be coloured in green as it has a positive connotation;
*   **2) Contrast —** checking the contrast between colours and a [colour and the background](https://coolors.co/contrast-checker/112a46-acc8e5) is important to ensure that the dashboard is visually comfortable and drives accessibility.

**Interactions** — think about the potential interactions the audience can have with charts (which are very dependent on the data visualisation platform you opt for) and how they can improve the data storytelling and exploration. Drill downs and tooltips can also help by adding data layers and context within the same space.

The tactics and tips above can drive you towards graphical excellence if applied individually, in each chart, and collectively, at the entire dashboard.

Building the entire dashboard with a close look to the details as much as for the charts will result in a consistent and cohesive product capable of delivering the most of the data to its audience.

**Important:**None of these techniques are truly impactful if not driving accessibility. There is an entire field dedicated to ensure accessibility in digital products and [for data products it shouldn’t be any different](https://home.vizlib.com/accessible-data-dashboard-designing/). Make sure to give it a look.

## Context, Context, Context

_Provide meaningful information for data interpretation_

Finally, once all charts are properly designed and positioned, make sure that the dashboard also includes the content which will support the audience to make the best out of it.

You can (and probably will) explain it a couple of times to stakeholders, but making sure that they can find their way through it in a self-serve manner definitely plays an important role in driving adoption and impact.

One way to concretely set it up is to **add a very complete and yet concise header to it** which:

*   includes a short description of the data presented;
*   mentions who is the audience of the dashboard;
*   presents disclaimers on the overall data (if any);
*   and references most relevant bookmarks (e.g. data glossaries, FAQ, other documentation, related dashboards)

**Keep in mind that —** Documentation, even though often neglected, should not be underestimated as it is a important resource to enrich readers’ knowledge asynchronously. In other words, good documentation can save everyone’s time and increase data literacy.

Finally, you can only be sure a dashboard delivers its best through testing and collecting feedback.

So, once you have finished its first version, walk the audience through it, ask them to take time to navigate it by themselves and share their feedback and questions that come up during the exploration. This should help you identify opportunities to improve the dashboard in different aspects — from analysis to design.

This post wasn’t written to serve as a complete guide on how to design excellent dashboards, but to surface (my) favourite steps and concepts when designing one.

I hope some of it sticks to you and that it inspires you to keep learning more about different techniques on dashboard design.
