So: is a dashboard necessary? Quick answer: no! But calm down, let me list the reasons and explain each of the points.

## Why a dashboard is not necessary

**1. Dashboards are tools.** As tools, they should aid in data-driven decision-making. On the other hand, they should not be the heart or central aspect of data-driven work conduct. As long as the decision is rational, preferably data-oriented, it is okay that there is not a dashboard in the play.

**2.** According to Stephen Few, Dashboard is a “Visual display of most of the information needed to achieve one or more objectives that fit entirely on a single computer screen so that it can be monitored at a glance”. **Therefore, dashboards are as useful as there is a situation to be monitored, evaluated, accompanied, and investigated (among other actions). But they are not the only way to make decisions.**

**3.** In the daily life of companies and corporations, dashboards are fundamentally exploratory tools. This means that **when we have to make a decision or meet with some decision maker, a presentation with storytelling (based on data!) is more convenient than a dashboard**, no matter how good a dashboard can be.

**4.** As we delve deeper into the knowledge and concepts of Data Literacy, we understand that **what matters is not how the team works with data, but that the team works well with data. That is, the important thing is for the professional to know how to work with data**, to know what a metric is, what a dimension is, when to use a centrality measure or a dispersion measure, when to use an aggregation of the mean type, the median type or sum, and so on. The way the professional prefers to deal with data is unimportant as long as he/she deals with data. So whether the data-oriented decision will come from a dashboard or a presentation with storytelling, it doesn’t matter.

Including, “dashboards are not necessary” so much that, according to Gartner (2019), dashboards as we know them are heading towards the end. As [Anderson Paes](https://www.linkedin.com/in/andersonpaes/), Information Designer at Oi, commented [here](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/andersonpaes_datadriven-datavisualization-dataviz-activity-6873259218053459968-LCUo/), the perception at that time was that presenting graphs is no longer enough: “Presenting large numbers does not reveal the scenario and still requires analysis time in a hyper-connected routine full of remote meetings. You will need to establish an analytical culture for this, to demonstrate the myriad insights that people will be able to glean from a crop of data.”

Indeed, Gartner was not the first to believe in a dashboardless future. Nor will it be the last, after all, [Steve MacFeely](https://www.linkedin.com/in/steve-macfeely-a1a710112/), Director of Data and Analytics of WHO (World Health Organization, in English) positioned himself in December 2022: “ [Dashboards are not the answer to everything](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/undataforum/blog/dashboards-are-not-the-answer-to-everything/) ”.

Having said that, however, it is important to highlight that today, what we see in the market is a belief in a [“Dashboardless” future](https://scribblr42.medium.com/the-future-of-dashboards-is-dashboardless-6f746ea7d850) (a term coined by [Ravi Mistry](https://scribblr42.medium.com/) in 2021).

## A dashboard is not necessary, but…

It is important to highlight, however, that a dashboard is not necessary, does not mean that it is not a good tool or that it does not help a lot in decision-making, or that it cannot facilitate — a lot! — data consumption and decision-making. On the contrary, a well-designed and built dashboard is a tool that can (too) facilitate your daily work.

I still remember an internal client story Anderson Paes shared with me: it was about an internal client who insisted that he needed a table on his dashboard. When the team went to figure out why, the customer wanted the results because he manually took the values for each of the records, calculated the average, and then compared each record’s value to the average. Something that a dashboard can already do automatically for the user!

A dashboard, when well built, saves the analyst’s time and speeds up decision-making and the identification of situations outside those imagined. But what would a well-built dashboard be? Simple: a dashboard that meets most of the user’s informational needs. Alternatively, following the “Pareto principle”, a well-built dashboard meets “80% of data analysis needs”.

To this end, at the time of construction, it is important to consider:

· The objective and the needs of the users

· Which metrics and KPIs are important for achieving the objective

· The existence or not of goals and reference values for the specified metrics

· The important dimensions for analysis

· The types of analysis important for the execution of the work activity

Furthermore, it is important to consider how users do their analyzes and their work routines. For example? There is no point in proposing a dashboard layout just for the notebook if the user who performs the analysis and/or who monitors the data rarely stops in front of the notebook and computer and, consequently, will consume the data mostly through the cell phone.

Thus, a well-built dashboard has everything to help a lot in data-oriented decision-making. So the user will spend less time on unnecessary activities such as performing calculations and/or complementary analyses, leaving more time to perform other activities with higher quality than the usual work.

## Conclusion

As Ravy Mistry himself wrote in his 2021 article ([The Future of Dashboards is Dashboardless](https://scribblr42.medium.com/the-future-of-dashboards-is-dashboardless-6f746ea7d850)), he coined the term from the concept of Serverless: serverless computing is not computing without a server, just computing with a server that it’s not always on, just when it needs to be). Even because, as Steve MacFeely wrote, “a panel can prepare us for emergencies, but it can also blind us against the unexpected. It can give us a false sense of security (…) If panels told us everything we need to know, there would be no traffic accidents”.

So, back to the initial question: is a dashboard necessary? No, it is not. It is even dangerous to rely too heavily on data panels. But definitely, your work activities will be better performed if you have a well-designed dashboard that helps you, instead of downloading extremely granular data and having to calculate all metrics and KPIs in Excel pivot tables.
