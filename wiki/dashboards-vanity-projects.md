# Are Data Dashboards Vanity Projects?

Data dashboards risk becoming vanity projects when users lack data literacy required to interpret visualizations. Numeracy, data literacy, and data visualization literacy form spectrum requiring progressively advanced skills. Without basic numeracy, individual cannot be data-literate, and visualizations reduce to decorative images. Research shows only 24% US workforce feels confident reading/working with data; less than 10% proficient in understanding numbers; Americans average numeracy level 2 of 5. Costly dashboards often yield low engagement from users lacking required literacy. Before investing in dashboard, consider users' actual literacy levels and whether automation/AI might better serve providing actionable insights.

## Key Patterns & Concepts

- **Numeracy**: Understanding, calculating, manipulating, interpreting results, communicating mathematical information; foundational for data literacy
- **Data Literacy**: Ability to read, analyze, work, and argue with data; builds on numeracy; requires critical thinking, sorting, filtering, standardizing data
- **Data Visualization Literacy**: Proficiency manipulating, interpreting data visualizations; requires high cognitive capacity; different visualization types need different cognitive effort
- **Data Complexity**: Messy, disorganized data from various sources; biased or incomplete data; challenging to understand; challenging to communicate
- **Cognitive Load**: Different visualization types require different cognitive capacities (bar graphs lower effort, network graphs convoluted); users comprehend basic charts/graphs better than maps/network layouts
- **Literacy Trends**: <10% general US population (age 16-65) proficient in understanding numbers; average proficiency level 2/5; low numeracy spawns low data literacy
- **Workforce Confidence**: Only 24% US workforce confident reading/working with/analyzing data; few feel comfortable working with data; many lack training time/resources
- **ROI Problem**: Dashboards costly to develop but yield low engagement; users lacking literacy can't decipher; often ineffective conveying clear actionable insights
- **Design Principle Contradiction**: UI shouldn't prioritize engagement over usability; satisfaction derives from efficient task completion not engagement duration; dashboards contradict both
- **When Visualizations Work**: Great for modeling known relationships, hypothetical outcomes between multiple variables, large quantities (help summarize relationships, identify trends)
- **Alternative Approach**: Instead of dashboards for general audiences, explore automation and AI providing actionable contextual insights throughout platform; capitalizes on technology investing in user engagement with potentially lower cost

What often overlooked: charts, graphs, other visuals can be merely decorations if users not data literate.

### Definitions & Concepts

To gauge people's understanding of data visualizations, essential defining concepts of numeracy and data literacy.

**Numeracy** is understanding, calculating, manipulating, interpreting results, communicating mathematical information. Involves reasoning, critical thinking, analyzing numerical data, making informed decisions based on quantitative information. Everyday concepts—date, chance, patterns, relationships, dimensions, shapes, numbers, change—form numeracy comprehension core. Numeracy lays groundwork for data literacy, which builds to data visualization literacy.

**Data Literacy** often interpreted as numeracy sub-type; summarized as ability reading, analyzing, working, and arguing with data. Visualizations present many difficulties for data workers; proficiency in such data manipulations coined as **data visualization literacy**.

Together, numeracy, data literacy, and data visualization literacy form spectrum where separating where one type begins/ends difficult. Without basic numeracy literacy, individual cannot be data-literate, and data visualizations reduce to decorative images.

### Complexity of Data

Exploring challenging aspects of working with data:

- **Data often messy and disorganized**: Comes from various sources requiring additional cleaning, verification, organization steps. Require critical thinking, ability sorting, filtering, standardizing data.
- **Data can be biased or incomplete**: Humans primary data creators/inspectors; results may be inaccurate or missing values.
- **Data challenging to understand**: Visualizations, statistical measurements, tables, spreadsheets represent variety formats. Different formats influence arriving at particular conclusions; correct interpretation requires high critical thinking degree.
- **Data challenging to communicate**: Presenters often rely on visual data rather than describing meaning and implications.

Reliance on data visualizations usually based on assumption they're intuitive. However, research shows not all data visualizations comprehensible. Different visualization types require different cognitive capacities. Bar graphs associated with lower cognitive effort; network graphs generally infrequent, convoluted, requiring users observing too many relations understanding.

### Data Literacy Trends

Numeracy, fundamental to data literacy, shows many proficiency/acquisition challenges, including many US people. Less than 10% general American population (16-65) proficient understanding numbers. Americans average numeracy proficiency level 2 out of 5, including performing basic calculations and interpreting simple tables/graphs. US ranks bottom compared other developed countries. Because numeracy and data literacy interconnected, low numeracy rates spawn low data literacy rates influencing many professional domains.

Although data become gold, any white-collar job expects significant data literacy, few feel comfortable working with data. Recent study found only 24% US workforce feels confident reading, working, analyzing, arguing with data. This lacking data literacy costs businesses millions dollars in lost productivity and efficiency; many businesses not providing adequate training.

Meanwhile, higher data visual literacy positively correlates making correct inferences from unfamiliar data visualizations. In data gold rush, we send people mining without proper tools. Result: data literacy remains main challenge in data management.

### Outlook & Recommendations

Given data literacy hurdles presenting usefulness challenges, dashboards tend being vanity projects. Questioning worth of most data visualization pages—extremely costly develop but usually earning low engagement from users. One UX principle: user engagement shouldn't come at usability expense. Another product design pillar: user satisfaction derives from efficient task completion rather than engagement duration. Data visualizations and dashboards tend contradicting both principles by foregrounding elements demanding engagement but presenting poor usability hindering platform efficiency, and generally not usable to most population.

To recover from this maldesign, recommend taking step back assessing reasons incorporating costly dashboard. Before investing building data visualizations/dashboards because seem necessary remaining competitive or that's what other B2B/B2C products doing, crucial considering users and their actual needs. While certain user profiles—data scientists, field scientists, researchers, medical professionals—possess advanced data literacy desiring visualization exploration freedom, most B2B and B2C users lack such proficiency.

For general audience, higher ROI return would exploring automation and AI technologies perhaps bridging data literacy gap by providing actionable contextual insights throughout platform. Such approach capitalizes platform technology investing in better user engagement with potentially lesser cost without relying on users' literacy.
