Dashboards. [**They are not always necessary**](https://medium.com/@antonioneto_17307/is-a-dashboard-necessary-a3153db47219). But they can help you a lot. And I already wrote here on Medium [**why and when this will happen**](https://medium.com/@antonioneto_17307/why-and-when-dashboards-will-help-you-1a7eccc9e452). What I haven’t written about yet — and this might surprise you — is that Dashboards aren’t the only types of data visualization products. If you believed they were, believe me, this text is for you. Otherwise, if you thought this was just another text about graphics, believe me, this text will surprise you as well.

To better understand the issue, however, let’s take a few steps back and remember: what is a data product? As [**I wrote here**](https://medium.com/@antonioneto_17307/what-is-a-data-product-cdd75f3c7ec9), it is “a product whose primary purpose is the use of data to facilitate an ultimate objective related to an informational need”. That said, a data product is not just a product built from data (such as the [**Netflix series House of Cards**](https://medium.com/@danial.a/how-netflix-used-data-to-create-house-of-cards-a-revolutionary-approach-to-content-creation-b9a114630ddc)) or a product that meets a need using data (such as the [**photo feed that the Instagram**](https://help.instagram.com/1986234648360433) models for each of its users). In my view, a data product is a product built from data and that uses data to meet an informational need.

This, however, does not mean that few things can be called as data product. On the contrary, there are countless data products. From the database built by the company’s data engineering area, to even a catalog that allows you to identify company’s data and indicators, all are data products. In fact, the structure of the company’s Data Analytics area impacts which data products will exist.

Do not believe? Take, for example, a company that has small data teams or data professionals who work in all business and structuring areas of the company, instead of having a Data Analytics directorate. This company will certainly have at least one data professional working in the Human Resources department. This professional will provide his managers with data from other teams, such as productivity and salary. However, it is unlikely that this company will make available to the rest of the company a product that indicates the cost of holding a meeting based on the proportional calculation of the salary of each professional called to that meeting. Even if this calls for reflection and rationalization about who should be involved in the meeting. Why? For the simple fact that the board that seeks to know about productivity (the famous HR) already has the information it needs, without needing the contribution of another board. There is a high chance that data will remain in silos in this case, which is not the case when you have a Data Analytics Department — with a commitment to generating data products for other areas of the company.

Having understood this, to understand which data visualization products are possible, we first need to understand what a data visualization product is. Here lies our first challenge. To understand why I say this, just remember, as I wrote in “ **Why and when Dashboards will help you** ”, that **Steve****Wexler, Jeffreu Shaffer & Andy Gotgreave** indicated in the book The Big Book of Dashboards (2017) the following: “Ask 10 people who build business dashboards for them to define a dashboard and you will probably have 10 different definitions.” Believe or not, the same goes for ‘data visualization’ — often known as visual analytics.

To give an example, **Thomas and Cook (2005)** define visual analysis as “the science of analytical reasoning facilitated by interactive visual interfaces”. According to the authors, visual analytics is a multidisciplinary field that includes areas of focus ranging from analytical reasoning techniques that allow users to obtain deep insights from data visualization, to visual representations and interaction techniques. According to this concept, as the authors themselves highlighted, the following areas of focus can still be included:

• data representations and transformations that convert all types of conflicting and dynamic data in ways that support visualization and analysis; It is

• techniques to support the production, presentation and dissemination of analytical results to communicate information in the appropriate context to a variety of audiences.

Following the line of Thomas & Cook, according to **Keim et al. (2006)**, visual analytics is more than just visualization and can be seen as an integrated approach that combines visualization, human factors and data analysis. For **Colin Ware (2000)**, however, the term visualization is something much simpler “a graphical representation of data or concepts”. Which, in itself, is quite a lot. After all, “presentations that utilize visual aids were found to be 43% more persuasive than presentations without aids,” according to a study by 3M and the University of Minnesota.

Therefore, it is important to highlight that in my view data visualization is nothing more than a visual analytical process. Process, as it often results from successive steps that result in the summary of a large volume of data into a set of actionable insights for quick decision making. Analytical because it is often the result of a set of techniques and complex data analysis. Visual for the simple fact of representing important results graphically in a way that makes them easier to understand.

In other words, Data Visualization is a form of graphic representation built from the use of creativity and design, and from the exploration of ideas, typography, shapes, colors, icons, and the knowledge of processes, data analysis, and cognitive decision-making processes to simplify, summarize, and make it easier to understand the meaning of data.

Therefore, I can categorically state that Dashboards are not the only data visualization products. On the contrary, data visualization products range from simple charts to infographics, diagrams, maps, data geolocation interfaces, visual analytical interfaces, dashboards, interactive analytical reports, narratives, data-based storytelling, among many other alternatives. As unusual examples of data visualization products, I also highlight the following:

• **Visual Alerts:** configurations of data visualization tools that trigger emails and announcements when a certain metric exceeds a stipulated level (whether higher or lower)

• **Visual Subscriptions:** configurations that periodically send images of dashboards and graphs to users via email, messaging applications such as WhatsApp and/or activity management tools, allowing the user to remain updated on data even without accessing the main product (the dashboard).

In my next article I will do something similar. I will write about different types of data visualization products and what informational needs they meet.

Until then, however, I invite you to remember what Keim et al (2008) indicated that the importance of visualization lies in the fact that its objective is to transform information overload into an opportunity so that decision makers can examine this huge flow of information and take effective action in real-time situations. This might help you think of visualization as something much broader than a dashboard, and look at many other data products as data visualization products.
