### At Projects by IF we believe that trust matters

When designing for trust we often talk about the distinction between trust and trustworthiness. Trust is a feeling given by people to organisations. Trustworthiness is a trait that organisations exhibit through how they provide a service. We believe it can be designed for, and have developed a [Responsible Technology by Design framework](https://medium.com/writing-by-if/updating-the-responsible-technology-by-design-framework-53a25bf94a3a) to do this.

### People want human support, but bad support breaks trust

“I just want to speak to someone” is a common utterance that occurs in both digital and non-digital services, especially when people are in distress or have urgent needs that require care. Unfortunately, [most systems use a first-come-first-served system](https://arc.net/l/quote/uepscgee) that does not consider case urgency.

Sometimes simply speaking to a human isn’t enough, especially when agents are not supported by proper training and robust processes. During a family emergency last year, I spent over an hour fighting an automated phone system just to reach a human. When I finally got through, I was passed from agent to agent without help. No one understood my situation or was able to solve it. In the end, I was sent to someone who didn’t speak my language. Even with people involved, the experience felt anything but human and only made my distress worse.

My example is extreme, but the same principles apply to most customer services:

*   People seek support when they have specific or sensitive needs that require careful consideration
*   People expect that speaking to a person means support will be more empathetic
*   People expect that speaking to a person means support can be situational and adaptive
*   Advice given by a person is perceived to be more accurate than advice given by an automated service (even though this isn’t always true)
*   Automated solutions have historically been too deterministic to provide an experience that feels human

### AI chatbots feel more human than ever before

Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled a quantum leap in the capabilities and accessibility of chatbots that ‘think’ and write just like people. This is great news for services creaking under backlogs of tickets that require repetitive intervention by human support agents. These new abilities are rapidly improving the perception of automated support journeys too. [A recent study by Zendesk](https://www.zendesk.co.uk/blog/ai-customer-service-statistics/) found that 50% of consumers think AI agents can be empathetic when addressing concerns.

For customer services, these advances mean that:

*   AI solutions can be adaptive to different contexts
*   AI solutions can tailor language to ensure people are treated with the dignity and respect they expect when seeking support
*   AI solutions can ‘learn’ and self-optimise without having to be completely re-developed
*   AI solutions can augment human services in ways that improve efficiency while ensuring people remain more in control than before

### Blind trust in AI chatbots is dangerous

Intelligent systems present the illusion of competence and use novelty to their advantage. They hide the seams in services and sacrifice transparency at the altar of ‘efficiency’.

When the mechanisms of responsibility, transparency or accountability are hidden, people can give too much trust to services that don’t deserve it — often at their peril. This is particularly dangerous when it comes to designing trustworthy chatbots that deliver support of any kind.[A recent study by Stanford](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/exploring-the-dangers-of-ai-in-mental-health-care) found that AI therapy chatbots contributed to harmful stigma and dangerous responses. People who have entered into committed relationships with AI partners on ChatGPT [have seen their relationships ruined](https://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2025/aug/16/chatgpt-update-love-boyfriend) following updates to the model. There’s even an emergent [‘AI-associated psychosis’](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/psych-unseen/202507/deification-as-a-risk-factor-for-ai-associated-psychosis), where deification of AI chatbots as a god-like form of super-intelligence can lead to psychosis.

### Transparency and control cannot be optional

When it comes to support, AI-enabled chatbots will undoubtedly continue to improve customer service for the majority of cases. But as we’ve seen, they also raise profound new challenges that need to be carefully considered.

Regardless of the underlying technology, organisations need to provide transparent, accountable and controllable solutions that empower users. The risks are real and the consequences are significant.

> For both policy and business teams alike, our design patterns are key in creating responsible AI-enabled services that serve people.

Perhaps extreme cases like the one I experienced will always need a capable human touch to resolve them. In the meantime, AI-enabled support agents will continue to get more capable. Head over to the [design patterns catalogue](https://catalogue.projectsbyif.com/#understanding-and-influencing-decisions) to learn how they can be designed to be trustworthy.

*   [**Show capabilities and limitations**](https://catalogue.projectsbyif.com/patterns/show-capabilities-and-limitations/)

This pattern is about helping users calibrate their trust before and while using a service. It’s important because we know that explaining what intelligent systems can and cannot do improves understanding and trustworthiness.
*   [**Offer task-level suggestions**](https://catalogue.projectsbyif.com/patterns/offer-task-level-suggestions/)

This pattern is about improving perception of AI through competence. It’s important because we know that people trust intelligent systems more when they remain in the driving seat.
*   [**Adapt to content and context**](https://catalogue.projectsbyif.com/patterns/adapt-to-content-and-context/)

This pattern is about making services more accessible and inclusive. It’s important because we know that timely support for those who need it most drastically improves outcomes.
