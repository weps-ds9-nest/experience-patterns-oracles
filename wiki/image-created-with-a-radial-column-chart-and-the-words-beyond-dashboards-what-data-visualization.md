When we talk about data visualization products, in general, the public already assumes that we are talking about Dashboards. There is no harm in that. After all, Dashboards are perhaps the best and biggest example of this type of product. However, as I wrote in my last article here on the network, there are others.

To understand this, just remember that data visualization is a way of presenting information that makes use of creativity, design, communication data analysis techniques, knowledge about biases, and people’s behavior, among other knowledge, to facilitate understanding of data through typography, colors, icons, and shapes. This brings us to our first big insight: data visualization products are nothing more than a subset of data products. Specifically, the subset of data products that makes use of a visual presentation to improve data understanding.

In fact, in the same way that we classify data products based on user needs, we can also group products associated with data visualization, as I indicated in the text [“What different data products can a company have?”](https://medium.com/@antonioneto_17307/what-different-data-products-can-a-company-have-2b130c35ea83). This means that data visualization products can also be classified according to the user’s needs. Specifically, however, data visualization products are normally associated only with the needs of data access and analysis, decision support, and discovery of ancillary information.

It is also interesting to highlight that this classification is not unique. On the contrary. In the same way that Wexler, Shaffer & Gotgreave indicated in the book The Big Book of Dashboards (2017) different classifications for Dashboards, which ranged from classification according to the business area with which it is related to classifications according to their type of use. There are many classifications for data visualization products. One of the simplest is whether the data visualization product in question is the main delivery made to the customer (as happens, for example, when we deliver a Dashboard) or whether it is an auxiliary delivery, an add-on (like, for example, documentation or a visual signature).

When we talk about objectives, the best classification, in my humble opinion, is that related to the user’s needs, as it is based on their informational needs that an individual searches for solutions and not based on domains of business areas or by a comparison between main products and add-ons products. Beyond ways to classify data visualization products, however, our goal here is to talk about some of these products, right? That said, the ones I know are:

**Charts**: These are the simplest data visualization products out there. They can be interactive or static and are associated with a very specific need that can be translated into a question. [Tableau’s Visual Vocabulary](https://www.tableau.com/solutions/gallery/visual-vocabulary), for example, presents different charts associated with your goals, which helps you understand which chart to use and when. In general, the need for access to data, data analysis and decision support, and also the discovery of information are associated. That’s why a good chart is always presented together with information about the primary source of the information.

**Infographics**: are presentations of information with a preponderance of graphic-visual elements (photography, drawing, statistical diagram, etc.) integrated into synthetic texts and numerical data, generally used as a complement or illustrative synthesis of information. They are, in general, associated with data access, data analysis, and decision support.

**Diagrams**: are visual representations of facts, phenomena, schemes, processes, and the like. They are not only necessarily related to data, but can also be used, for example, to illustrate how data transformation processes take place. Even when we talk about data architecture, in general, there is always a diagram being delivered to illustrate the flow of data until it reaches the user.

**Data Geolocation Maps and Interfaces**: These are graphical representations, on a reduced scale, of the total surface and the data associated with these surfaces. The most common are those that simplify the surface as it is, but there are countless other types of maps such as distorted maps, among others. Such as graphs are associated with access to data, data analysis, and support for decision-making, especially when we are talking about data with spatial correlation such as the distribution of wealth.

**Dashboards**: As defined by Stephen Few, a dashboard is a “visual display of the information most needed to achieve one or more objectives, which fits entirely on a single computer screen so that it can be monitored at a glance”. I wrote some texts related to these products, so I suggest a look at my profile [here](https://medium.com/@antonioneto_17307) to better understand these products. It is worth mentioning that their most common classification is that which indicates that they can be strategic, analytical, or operational. In general, the needs for obtaining, transforming, accessing, and analyzing data, supporting decisions, and discovering information are associated.

**Analytical Reports**: Very similar to Dashboards in terms of the use of graphs (that is, both are based on a group of two or more graphs). However, they differ in that they are not so much an exploratory tool as an explanatory tool. This means that they bring with them as much text as necessary and that they already present the data with an explanatory thread, obtained after analyzing the data. In general, they are associated with access to data, data analysis, and decision support, and with the discovery of information.

**Data-based narratives and storytelling**: Like analytical reports, they are heavily based on graphics and have a single thread. However, as they do not have as much text, they are less explanatory and more exhibition tools. They are generally constructed for presentations to support the explanation of the data and suggested decision-making being presented.

**Alerts and Subscriptions**: configurations of visualization tools that trigger emails and announcements, either periodically or when a certain metric exceeds a stipulated level (either higher or lower). These are auxiliary-type products that, in general, are delivered together with other graphs and/or dashboards. Images can be sent via email, messaging applications such as WhatsApp, and/or activity management tools, and allow the user to remain updated on data even without accessing the main product.

It is also worth highlighting that all of these products mentioned above, regardless of whether they are main products or whether they are add-ons, must be delivered together with documentation. This documentation must explain in detail what they are, what data is used, the source, latency, and periodicity of the data, and must be accompanied by indications of who is responsible for both the product and the data. These indications are related to the much-needed concepts of transparency and data governance. Believe me: if you think that having well-explained documentation is not enough, wait until the first time you need to migrate a product from one platform to another, and no one has the knowledge about business intelligence or data analysis techniques to explain to you how things should be done on the new platform.

Finally, it is important, however, to differentiate the data visualization product here from the tools offered by companies that sell data visualization solutions. The fact that a data visualization product is not conducive to data transformation does not mean, however, that large companies such as Tableau, PowerBI, or Qlik do not have within their suites products or functionalities that allow them to transform data.
