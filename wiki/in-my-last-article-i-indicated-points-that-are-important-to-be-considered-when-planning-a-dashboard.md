Today, however, I want to show you why a well-designed panel is a differential, and, mainly, the differential of a well-designed panel.

**But before**, let’s encourage our recent memory recovery: **what elements should we consider to be closer to building a well-designed dashboard**? As I wrote in my last article: 1) the person(s) consuming the data, 2) the way the data is consumed, 3) the purpose for which the data is consumed, 4) the analysis performed upon that data, 5) the actions that will be taken and the person(s) impacted by the information obtained from the data, and 6) the context in which the data is in

If you think about it, you will notice that most of the points indicated show us that a well-designed and built data dashboard is not just a data-centric product. Even before that, it is (or should be) a people-oriented product. And this is based on a simple fact: if a good dashboard translates data analysis techniques (sometimes routine, sometimes complex) and a large volume of data into simple visualizations that make efficient decisions in a relationship, a good data panel is nothing more than a tool that makes someone’s work easier. In other words, a good dashboard is built to be part of someone’s work routine.

That said, you won’t be able to build a good dashboard that meets the analytical prerequisites if you don’t invite the business user to participate in the creation process with you at least at some stage. Because no one knows better than the user himself (herself) the activity (s)he needs to do, the analysis (s)he develops, the data (s)he usually monitors, and how (s)he usually monitors it. Note, therefore, that the great differential of a well-designed panel is precisely that it is thought out and planned together with the user, the individual who will make use of that data analysis interface.

Note that I’m not saying that you can’t create a dashboard with a superior design, with innovative and extremely interesting visuals, or that performs very complex analysis. Yes, it is possible. And it is quite possible that if you are a very good professional in data visualization, you can build a good dashboard without consulting the user, and that it is too good. Or, as I like to say, good enough. However, no matter how good it is, there is a great chance that it is not the tool that facilitates the user’s activity, and that will make the user perform the best analysis for his department, sector, and company.

**And why is a well-designed panel a differential?** To answer that question I’m going to turn to something Steve Jobs said in the past: “Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works”. By bringing this point to the reality of a visual dashboard, if you consider the user’s needs when creating the dashboard design, then you will ensure that it will be used and that good results will be achieved.

If this example were not enough to show why a dashboard with superior design is a differentiator, then it is worth remembering the words of Robert L. Peter: “Design creates culture. Culture shapes values. Values determine the future”. And in a society that increasingly realizes the value of being data-driven, it is important to indicate that design can help your company adhere to a data-driven culture and thus make more rational decisions in search of a better result. After all, a good panel design will transform complex analyzes and recurring activities into something simple to be consumed by the user, helping in decision-making. And a user who performs his activities more easily and in less time has time and energy to carry out other activities — sometimes even more important ones!

There is no way, therefore, in this sense, to remember the words of Thomas Watson Jr: “Good design is good business”. Not in the sense and context in which he conceived them, it is true, but which does not make the phrase any less true. After all, investing in good design, as I demonstrated above, is good business. And even more than that: it is to guarantee a good deal. Do you agree?
