**Why Does Psychology Matter in Data Visualization?**

 Imagine a manager analyzing a dashboard filled with complex charts where no information clearly stands out. They might feel overwhelmed, make mistakes, or even abandon the dashboard altogether. Conversely, a dashboard designed with psychological principles in mind can intuitively and efficiently guide users to relevant insights.

**The Role of Cognitive Biases**

 Cognitive biases directly influence how data is interpreted. Some of the most relevant for data visualization include:

*   **Anchoring Bias:** Users may focus excessively on the first data point they see, even if it’s not the most relevant. This is why it’s crucial to design dashboards where critical data is highlighted, such as in a “Key KPIs” section.
*   **Cognitive Overload:** Presenting too much information can confuse users, making it difficult to identify key insights. A classic example is a dashboard with dozens of charts and no clear hierarchy or grouping, as illustrated below.

The image shows an example of bad dashboard with a lot of charts. Building a great dashboard is also a matter of choice: you don’t to put the data or all the possible analyses — only the important ones to take actions and make decisions!

*   **Confirmation Bias:** Poorly designed dashboards can reinforce preconceived notions. For instance, a sales performance dashboard that only displays the best-selling products might lead users to overlook opportunities in underperforming products.

By understanding these biases, we can design visualizations that help users avoid cognitive pitfalls and make more informed decisions. This includes practices such as limiting the amount of information per page and using visual elements to encourage critical analysis.

**Visual Perception and Hierarchy**

 Psychology also influences how our eyes and brains process visual information. Principles like Gestalt help us understand how people group visual elements:

*   **Proximity:** Elements placed close together are perceived as related. For example, in a dashboard, sales and marketing charts can be placed near each other to suggest a correlation between these data sets.
*   **Similarity:** Visually similar elements are automatically grouped. Consistent colors for similar categories enable users to quickly understand connections between data points.

Additionally, designing dashboards that follow consistent mental models, so users always know where to find filters or key takeaways, can significantly enhance the user experience. Studies on visual reading patterns often show that users follow “F” or “Z” patterns when exploring dashboards. Designing layouts that align with these patterns can make the experience more seamless and intuitive.

**Color Psychology**

 Colors are not merely aesthetic; they evoke emotions and guide interpretation. For example:

*   **Red** can indicate alerts or danger, such as identifying issues in critical metrics.
*   **Green** is associated with success or approval, making it ideal for highlighting KPIs that meet targets.

However, excessive use of colors or high contrast can confuse users. Imagine a dashboard where all charts are red, green, and yellow — at the same time. Or a panel with ten categories, each represented by a different color. This visual overload can make it harder to identify patterns. The solution lies in limiting the number of colors and using them consistently to create a pleasant and functional visual experience, always emphasizing what’s most important in the analysis.

**Connecting Data Visualization and Psychology in Practice**

 Considering everything discussed, how can we practically connect data visualization and psychology? Below are some well-known examples, though there are many others. Rest assured, we will explore all of them throughout 2025.

*   **Planning:** Understand your audience and their needs. A dashboard for executives should be straightforward, while one for analysts can include more details.
*   **Expectation vs. Reality:** Everyone who has worked in Data & Analytics knows that delivering a data product is not always easy or fast. Use ideation and prototyping techniques to set clear expectations with clients during the development process.
*   **Usability Testing:** Test your dashboard with real users to identify areas of confusion or opportunities for improvement.
