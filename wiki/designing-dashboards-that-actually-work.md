# Designing Dashboards That Actually Work

## Simple UX principles for creating data visualizations

Generated by Stablecog

If you ever feel like data is trying to drown you, trust me, you’re not alone. These days, everything’s dripping with data, and if you want your work to actually get through to people, you’ve got to make that data _look_ good and, more importantly, _make sense_. Nobody has the patience to scroll through endless spreadsheets that look like ancient hieroglyphics. A good chart is like a superhero cape for your data, it saves the day by turning boring numbers into a story everyone can get. But here’s the catch: if you pick the wrong chart, your audience will just stare blankly and wonder if they accidentally signed up for a math exam instead of your presentation.

Think about your car’s dashboard. You don’t want it cluttered with random knobs and flashing lights, right? You want just the essentials, easy to glance at while you’re stressing over traffic. That’s exactly what your dashboard should do: show the important stuff clearly, not make people reach for their glasses.

Here’s a cheat sheet for making dashboards that are clear, useful, and maybe even a little fun, without putting your audience to sleep.

## Know your people

Before spitting charts to all directions, figure out what your data is _actually_ saying and who’s going to see it. Dashboards aren’t meant for everyone. They’re like custom coffee orders: one size definitely does not fit all. Your dashboard should tell one clear story. Don’t cram everything in or your viewers will leave more confused than when they started.

## Pick simple charts and ditch the fancy stuff

The wrong chart is like a bad haircut. Everyone notices and no one wants to admit it.

*   Avoid dual-axis charts: they’re basically the data equivalent of watching a Christopher Nolan movie without paying much attention. In the end, you don’t understand half of it
*   Keep pie charts to 5 or 7 slices; if you have more, go for a bar chart instead. Nobody likes squinting at a donut chart thinner than the free coffee at the office.
*   3D charts with shadows and gradients? Nope. Pretty sure they were invented to distract you from what you’re actually supposed to learn.
*   Vertical bar charts always start the y-axis at zero, or you risk looking like someone trying to tweaking the numbers.
*   If all else fails, sometimes just showing one big bold number is the smartest move. It’s like flashing a neon sign that says, “This is the important thing, pay attention!”

## Cut the clutter like a pro

Clutter is the enemy of clarity. Tackle it like you’re cleaning out your desk drawer. If you don’t need it, toss it.

Less text often means more understanding. Sounds weird, but fewer words let people get the point faster.

Dump unnecessary gridlines, ticks, and borders. And ditch those chart legends whenever you can. They’re like having to look back and forth in a boring dance-off. Just label right on the chart and save us all a headache.

## Make important info pop

People skim dashboards like they skim Netflix titles. Really fast.

Guide their attention to the star of the show. Highlight things carefully. If you make everything shout, you just get a noisy crowd with no clear lead singer.

Use bold text wisely and line up your elements cleanly. Good spacing and white space aren’t for filling gaps, they’re like breathing room that lets your data relax and look sharper.

## Color and fonts that don’t hurt people’s eyes

Make sure your colors pop in all the right ways, with good contrast so no one needs a microscope.

Sans-serif fonts are the cool kids in the font world, easy to read and friendly. Please, no yelling with ALL CAPS, and don’t make your text do the limbo by rotating it sideways.

If labels are too long, horizontal bar charts can save the day. And for every chart, slap on a clear title and axis labels so nobody’s stuck guessing what’s what. If you’re presenting, try throwing in action titles that say what the takeaway is, upfront and loud.

## Make it easy enough for your grandma

Your dashboard should be for everyone, not just stats nerds. If it looks scary or complicated, people will run faster than you can say “data visualization.”

The best way to win the crowd is to test it. Get real users to poke around and share what’s confusing them.

## In sources we trust

Be honest about your data’s origins. Link to the original sources, dates, and if you can, let folks download the raw stuff, if possible. Transparency builds trust, and trust means people actually pay attention.

By following these down-to-earth tips, you’ll create dashboards that actually help people, not just look good on screen. Data visualization is both a science and an art. Keep your focus on clear communication and let the data tell the story, not fancy effects or stuffing in every chart type you know.
